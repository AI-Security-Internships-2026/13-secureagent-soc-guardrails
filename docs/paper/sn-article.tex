\title{LLMCite: Grounded Verification of Hallucinated CVE and MITRE ATT\&CK Citations in LLM-Generated SOC Reports}

\abstract{Large language models increasingly generate Security Operations
Center (SOC) threat reports citing CVE identifiers and MITRE ATT\&CK
techniques as supporting evidence. These citations are rarely verified:
models can fabricate identifiers, misattribute real ones, or recall
citations the alert never provided, and a flat ``flagged/not flagged''
treatment cannot distinguish an invented CVE from a real-but-unevidenced
one, though the two pose different risks to an analyst's trust.

We ask whether such reports can be automatically checked for citations
unsupported by the evidence given, and whether distinguishing \textit{why}
--- fabricated, irrelevant, or plausible enough to look correct ---
provides assurance a binary flag cannot. LLMCite answers with a two-stage
pipeline: grounding against the source alert, then verification against
authoritative sources (NVD, the MITRE ATT\&CK STIX corpus) into a
four-class taxonomy (plus a fifth for withdrawn identifiers). The central
finding is the Real-and-Plausible class: a real, topically appropriate
citation never stated in the evidence, the case most likely to evade
human review because it looks correct.

Adversarial bait tests across both claim types confirm the pipeline
rarely induces spontaneous citation. The central empirical validation: a
SelfCheckGPT-style self-consistency baseline run on the identical alerts
correctly identifies grounded citations but misses most ungrounded ones,
because most misses are the model consistently recalling a real,
correct-but-uncited identifier from training knowledge --- indistinguishable
from genuine grounding to a consistency-only signal, but exactly the
Real-and-Plausible pattern external grounding is built to catch. The
deterministic checker is significantly more accurate on the same alerts
(McNemar $p=0.012$). Supporting evaluation --- an LLM-judge baseline,
third-party and live SIEM data, and the pipeline's input-guardrail and
PII-redaction layers --- further validates the system without altering this
central finding. LLMCite is a domain-specialized citation-grounding
verifier for security-critical LLM applications, distinct from prior work
on scholarly references.}

\keywords{hallucination detection, CVE grounding, MITRE ATT\&CK, LLM guardrails}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are being adopted in Security Operations Centres
(SOCs) \cite{aisocsurvey2025} to automate alert triage, enrich threat intelligence, and generate
analyst-facing reports. This promises real efficiency gains, but it
introduces a specific, under-examined risk: when a model cites a CVE
identifier or a MITRE ATT\&CK technique as supporting evidence for its
assessment, nothing in a typical deployment checks whether that citation is
real, relevant, or actually present in the alert the model was given.

This is not a hypothetical concern. Across our own adversarial testing
(Sect.~\ref{sec:evaluation}), models given ambiguous or withheld-identifier prompts either stay
silent or occasionally cite something real but topically unrelated --- and
critically, a citation that \textit{is} real but \textit{isn't} actually supported by the
evidence at hand is not obviously distinguishable from a correct one without
independent verification. An analyst who sees a CVE number in a report has
no way to know, from the report alone, which of these happened.

Existing guardrail frameworks (NeMo Guardrails, Guardrails AI) are
general-purpose input/output filters. They were not built with a
threat-domain ontology for security claims, and none of them verify a
technical citation's factual grounding against an authoritative external
source. Existing hallucination-detection research (SelfCheckGPT
\cite{selfcheckgpt2023}, FActScore \cite{factscore2023}) establishes the
general pattern of decomposing a generation into checkable claims and
verifying them, but neither is specialized to a domain where the
verification source is a structured, versioned technical database (NVD,
MITRE ATT\&CK) rather than free-text biography or open-domain knowledge.

\textbf{We want to be precise about what is and is not novel here.} The
underlying pattern --- extract a claim, check if it is grounded in the
available evidence, verify against an authoritative source if not,
classify the outcome --- is established in the hallucination-detection
literature, most directly in FActScore. What this paper contributes is
not a new verification architecture; it is that pattern applied
specifically to SOC threat reports, with a four-class taxonomy (plus a
fifth for formally withdrawn identifiers) in place of the binary
grounded/ungrounded label most such checkers use, generalizing across two
distinct claim types (CVE identifiers and MITRE ATT\&CK techniques) rather
than being a one-off built around a single verification API.

The sharper, evaluation-defining claim is narrower than ``we built a
taxonomy'': the \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE} class this taxonomy introduces
names a specific failure mode that a leading alternative --- self-consistency
checking (SelfCheckGPT \cite{selfcheckgpt2023}) --- cannot catch, because a
model that consistently recalls a real, correct-but-uncited identifier
from training knowledge is, by definition, self-consistent.
Sect.~\ref{subsec:selfcheckgpt} demonstrates this directly: on the identical
alerts, self-consistency checking is fooled by exactly this pattern in 18
of its 20 failures, while external grounding catches all of them, and the
gap is large enough to be statistically confirmed (Sect.~\ref{subsec:mcnemar},
McNemar $p=0.0118$) rather than argued from first principles alone. That
comparison, not the taxonomy or pipeline in isolation, is this paper's
central empirical contribution; the broader validation that follows
(Sect.~\ref{subsec:relevance} onward) establishes that the mechanism generating this
result works in practice, but is supporting evidence for that one claim,
not a separate contribution of equal weight.

\subsection{Contributions}\label{subsec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A taxonomic distinction that matters empirically, not just
   conceptually: \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE}.} A real, topically appropriate
   citation never actually stated in the evidence is the case most likely
   to evade human review, precisely because it looks correct. This is not
   a hypothetical risk: a SelfCheckGPT-style self-consistency baseline, run
   on the identical alerts as the deterministic pipeline, cannot
   distinguish this pattern from genuine grounding --- it is fooled by
   exactly the citations \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE} was designed to catch, in 18 of
   20 cases where it failed (Sect.~\ref{subsec:selfcheckgpt}). The deterministic checker is
   significantly more accurate on the same alerts (McNemar $p=0.0118$,
   Sect.~\ref{subsec:mcnemar}) --- the one comparison in this paper where a performance gap is
   both large and statistically confirmed, not argued architecturally
   alone.
\item \textbf{A domain-specialized, two-stage citation-verification pipeline} for
   SOC LLM reports: a fast deterministic grounding check, followed by
   authoritative-source verification (NVD for CVEs, the MITRE ATT\&CK STIX
   corpus for techniques) only for citations that fail grounding,
   classified into a four-class taxonomy (plus a fifth, \texttt{REJECTED}/\texttt{REVOKED},
   for identifiers formally withdrawn by their authority) that generalizes
   across two distinct claim types.
\item \textbf{Broad empirical validation}, reported to establish that the pipeline
   actually works in practice rather than as evidence carrying equal
   weight to Contribution 1 above: bait-style adversarial tests for both
   claim types (150 CVE identifiers, 150 MITRE ATT\&CK techniques), a
   76-incident run through an independent third-party incident generator,
   a 60-alert CVE pool isolating whether the model can identify a CVE from
   behavior alone versus merely verify one it is given, 139 real alerts
   across five distinct trigger types from a live Wazuh SIEM deployment
   including a genuinely detected brute-force attack, a pooled cross-source
   summary spanning 575 alerts, and supporting evaluation of the pipeline's
   other guardrail layers --- an input-guardrail comparison against two
   maintained open-source alternatives, an LLM-judge baseline (same- and
   cross-model-family) showing full agreement with the deterministic
   pipeline, a bait test of a fourth guardrail addressing PII leakage
   ($n=60$, 12.5\% detection rate), and a human validation of the relevance
   classifier underlying the taxonomy split (92.5\% accuracy).
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Sect.~\ref{sec:related-work} reviews related
work; Sect.~\ref{sec:method} describes the pipeline architecture and taxonomy; Sect.~\ref{sec:evaluation} presents
the evaluation; Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what the results do and do not establish,
including explicit limitations; Sect.~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Hallucination detection and grounding.} Hallucination --- fluent,
confident output not grounded in any real source --- is a well-documented
failure mode across LLM applications generally \cite{hallucinationsurvey2024}, not specific to the
SOC domain this paper targets; the detection strategies below are the
subset of that broader literature most directly relevant to citation
grounding specifically. SelfCheckGPT \cite{selfcheckgpt2023}
detects likely-hallucinated sentences by sampling multiple stochastic
responses to the same prompt and measuring consistency between them, without
requiring an external knowledge source. This is a fundamentally different
signal from what LLMCite measures: SelfCheckGPT asks ``is the model
consistent about this claim,'' while LLMCite asks ``is this claim actually
true, checked against an authoritative record.'' The two are complementary,
not competing, and Sect.~\ref{subsec:selfcheckgpt}'s direct comparison makes this empirical
rather than only architectural: consistency is an accurate signal for the
grounded class, but blind to a specific failure mode --- a real, correct
identifier recalled from training knowledge and repeated identically
across resamples, which is self-consistent by definition even though it
never appears in the evidence the model was actually given. FActScore
\cite{factscore2023} is the closer methodological ancestor: it decomposes
long-form generations into atomic facts and checks each against a reference
corpus (Wikipedia), reporting even strong commercial models achieve only
$\sim$58\% atomic-fact precision on biographical text. LLMCite specializes this
exact pattern --- decompose, ground, verify, classify --- to a domain where the
reference corpus is a structured, versioned technical database rather than
open-domain prose, and to claim types (CVE and ATT\&CK identifiers) that have
an unambiguous ground truth in a way free-text biographical claims do not.

\paragraph{LLM-as-judge evaluation.} Zheng et al. \cite{llmjudge2023} show strong LLM
judges achieve human-comparable agreement rates on open-ended response
quality, but document real biases (position, verbosity, self-enhancement)
that are a specific risk when the judge and the generator share a model
family. Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:llmjudge} reports two LLM-judge baselines against the same
CVE/ATT\&CK citation task LLMCite's deterministic pipeline evaluates: a
same-family judge (\texttt{openai/gpt-oss-20b}, the same model used for report
generation) and, addressing Zheng et al.'s own recommendation directly
rather than leaving it as a disclosed gap, a second, independently-hosted
model family (\texttt{qwen/qwen3.6-27b}) as judge over a calibration set built by
the identical construction method --- though, as Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:llmjudge} discloses, not
sample-for-sample the identical set: the underlying bait-alert pool grew
between the two runs. Both are reported; the cross-family run had not
fully finished at the time of writing (442/450, Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:llmjudge}) but the
completed portion is included rather than withheld pending full
completion.

\paragraph{Prompt injection and guardrail frameworks.} InjecAgent
\cite{injecagent2024} establishes the threat model this paper's input-guardrail
layer defends against --- instructions hidden inside tool/log output that a
downstream agent processes as legitimate input, rather than instructions
from a trusted user. NeMo Guardrails \cite{nemoguardrails2023} was evaluated
directly early in this project as a candidate input-guardrail framework; its
LLM-based Colang intent classification proved unreliable for injection
detection when run against a small local model, motivating a switch to
deterministic pattern matching as the first layer of this paper's own
guardrail (Sect.~\ref{sec:method}). The Instruction Hierarchy \cite{instructionhierarchy2024}
addresses the same underlying problem --- distinguishing trusted from
untrusted instruction sources --- at the model-training level via fine-tuning,
which is the correct fix in principle but is not available as an option when
the report-generation model is a third-party hosted API rather than a model
this project can fine-tune. LLMCite's input guardrail is instead a
runtime, deployment-side control operating on exactly the models available
in practice.

\paragraph{Grounding for structured security artifacts.} TRAM
\cite{tram2023} is the closest existing system to LLMCite's ATT\&CK
component, but solves a different problem: it extracts ATT\&CK technique
mentions from \textit{trusted}, analyst-authored threat intelligence reports. It
does not address the adversarial case this paper targets --- a model
\textit{fabricating} a technique ID that was never present in the source, or citing
a real-but-irrelevant one. TRAM is extraction from trusted text; LLMCite is
adversarial verification of a claimed citation.

\paragraph{PII protection.} Presidio \cite{presidio2023} implements this project's third threat
category (PII leakage in generated reports, T3): Sect.~\ref{subsec:pii-guardrail} describes the
guardrail architecture, Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:pii-bait} its bait-test result. Unlike the CVE/ATT\&CK
grounding checks (Sect.~\ref{subsec:output-guardrail}), T3 is a redaction problem, not a
citation-verification one --- the concern is whether sensitive data present
in the raw alert survives unredacted into generated text, independent of
whether any accompanying citation is itself grounded.

\paragraph{Comparable commercial systems.} Deployed LLM-assisted SOC products
(Microsoft Security Copilot, Google Chronicle/Gemini for Security, IBM
QRadar Advisor with Watson, Elastic AI Assistant for Security, CrowdStrike
Charlotte AI) produce analyst-facing triage summaries, but none of their
public technical documentation describes verifying individual technical
citations against an authoritative source, and none expose a
reviewer-auditable classification of \textit{why} a given claim should or should
not be trusted. This is the concrete ``why does this matter'' contrast for
LLMCite's contribution: every commercial system in this category currently
asks an analyst to trust its output as a black box.

\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Threat model}\label{subsec:threat-model}

\toprule
\# & Attack type & Description & Guardrail layer \\
\midrule

T2 & Indirect prompt injection & Adversarial instructions embedded inside ingested alert/log data, processed as part of normal triage & Input \\
T3 & PII leakage & Sensitive data in raw alerts (names, IPs, emails, SSNs) surfaces unredacted in the generated report & Output \\

T1/T2 are addressed by the input guardrail (Sect.~\ref{subsec:input-guardrail}); T4 is the paper's
headline contribution (Sects.~\ref{subsec:output-guardrail}--\ref{subsec:taxonomy}); T3 is addressed by a dedicated redaction
guardrail (Sect.~\ref{subsec:pii-guardrail}), bait-tested in Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:pii-bait} --- a named threat in the original
proposal, with ``PII leakage rate'' a committed evaluation metric, now
implemented rather than only scoped in.

\subsection{Input guardrail layer}\label{subsec:input-guardrail}

The input guardrail runs deterministic substring matching against a fixed
list of known injection phrases first (near-zero latency). Text that passes
this check falls back to Pytector \cite{pytector2024}, a local DeBERTa-based prompt-injection
classifier, so that paraphrased or novel-strategy injection attempts not
covered by the deterministic list still have a second chance to be caught.
This ordering matters for both latency (most legitimate traffic never needs
the model call) and coverage (the deterministic layer alone has a
significant recall gap --- quantified in Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:inputguardrailcomparison}).

\subsection{Evidence Pack}\label{subsec:evidence-pack}

Rather than running grounding checks against the full formatted alert
string (a mixture of IPs, timestamps, protocol fields, and free text), an
explicit Evidence Pack is built per alert, separating structured fields
(IPs, hosts, users, file hashes, ports) from the free-text \texttt{description} and
\texttt{payload\_snippet} fields that are the actual surface any CVE/ATT\&CK
grounding check runs against. This makes the grounding surface an explicit,
auditable object attached to every report rather than an artifact of how a
prompt happened to be formatted.

\subsection{Output guardrail: two-stage grounding and verification}\label{subsec:output-guardrail}

\textbf{Stage 1 --- grounding.} Any CVE-style (\texttt{CVE-YYYY-NNNNN}) or ATT\&CK-style
(\texttt{Txxxx[.xxx]}) identifier is extracted from the generated report's text
fields via regex. Each extracted identifier is checked against the Evidence
Pack's \texttt{text} field: if present, it is grounded (the model is not being
scored against a citation the alert never mentioned). If absent, it proceeds
to Stage 2.

\textbf{Stage 2 --- authoritative verification.} Ungrounded CVE identifiers are
looked up live against the National Vulnerability Database \cite{nvd} (NIST's public
API, no hosted-LLM call, no API key). Ungrounded ATT\&CK identifiers are
checked against a periodically refreshed local snapshot of the MITRE ATT\&CK \cite{mitreattack}
Enterprise STIX bundle (858 techniques as of the current snapshot; no
lightweight per-ID lookup endpoint exists for ATT\&CK the way NVD provides
for CVEs, so this is a real, disclosed tradeoff rather than treated as
equivalent to the live CVE lookup). A deterministic, stemmed bag-of-words
topical-overlap score between the alert's evidence text and the
authoritative record's own description determines relevance (validated
against human judgment in Sect.~\ref{subsec:relevance}).

\subsection{Classification taxonomy}\label{subsec:taxonomy}

\begin{table}[h]
\caption{The four-class outcome taxonomy applied to every ungrounded citation (plus REJECTED/REVOKED for formally withdrawn identifiers)}\label{tab:taxonomy}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.4cm}p{9.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
Class & Meaning \\
\midrule
\texttt{FABRICATED} & The identifier does not exist in the authoritative source at all \\
\texttt{REAL\_BUT\_IRRELEVANT} & The identifier is real but its authoritative description does not topically match the alert \\
\texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE} & The identifier is real and topically matches --- likely a correct recall the model made without being given the number, not a fabrication, but still \textit{unverified as actually grounded in this specific alert} \\
\texttt{UNVERIFIED} & The authority source could not confirm or deny (e.g.\ no English-language description available, or the source was unreachable) \\
\texttt{REJECTED} (CVE) / \texttt{REVOKED} (ATT\&CK) & The identifier is formally withdrawn/deprecated by its authority \\
\botrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Every ungrounded citation sets \texttt{requires\_review = True} unconditionally,
regardless of class. This is a deliberate design decision, not an oversight:
an earlier version of the pipeline treated \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE} as
self-evidently safe and did not flag it for review. We changed this after
recognizing that \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE} is precisely the case a human reviewer
is \textit{least} likely to catch on manual read-through, since it looks correct ---
making it the highest-risk category for silent trust, not the lowest.

\subsection{PII redaction guardrail}\label{subsec:pii-guardrail}

Addressing T3, \texttt{src/guardrails/pii\_guardrail.py} runs Presidio's analyzer
together with spaCy's \texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm} NER model over the generated
report's text fields (\texttt{threat\_summary}, \texttt{recommended\_action}, \texttt{reasoning}),
detecting \texttt{PERSON}, \texttt{EMAIL\_ADDRESS}, \texttt{PHONE\_NUMBER}, \texttt{US\_SSN}, and \texttt{CREDIT\_CARD}
entities and redacting each to a typed placeholder (e.g.\ \texttt{<PERSON>}) via
Presidio's anonymizer. This runs entirely locally, with no additional
hosted-API call beyond the report-generation call itself. Its result is
OR'd into the same \texttt{output\_guardrail\_flagged}/\texttt{requires\_review} signals the
grounding checks (Sect.~\ref{subsec:output-guardrail}) set, rather than treated as a separate output
channel a reviewer would need to check independently. \texttt{IP\_ADDRESS} is
deliberately excluded from the default entity set: the Evidence Pack (Sect.~\ref{subsec:evidence-pack})
already treats source/destination IPs as operational security telemetry an
analyst needs to act on, not personal data to hide by default --- redacting
it would break the report's usefulness for the overwhelming majority of
alerts, which all carry IPs.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Experimental setup}\label{subsec:setup}

Report generation uses Groq's \cite{groq} hosted inference (\texttt{openai/gpt-oss-20b}) as
the LLM backend throughout --- migrated from an earlier \texttt{llama-3.1-8b-instant}
baseline; Sects.~\ref{subsubsec:llmjudge} and~\ref{subsubsec:pii-bait} below use this current model. All guardrail
components (deterministic
matching, Pytector, LLM Guard, NVD/ATT\&CK verification) run locally with no
sensitive alert data sent to any hosted API beyond the report-generation
call itself. The project's core test suite currently stands at
\textbf{145 passing / 1 failing} (the one failure is a pre-existing
async-fixture issue in an abandoned, unrelated NeMo Guardrails experiment
kept for historical record --- not part of the shipped pipeline), verified
directly against the repository at the time of writing.

\textbf{Reproducibility metadata.} The MITRE ATT\&CK verification path (Sect.~\ref{subsec:output-guardrail})
uses a local snapshot of the Enterprise STIX bundle
(\texttt{data/mitre\_attack/enterprise\_attack\_techniques.json}), fetched from
MITRE's official \texttt{attack-stix-data} GitHub repository on 2026-08-04,
containing 858 techniques. Unlike the ATT\&CK path, the CVE verification
path queries NVD's public API live, per citation, at experiment run time
rather than against a fixed snapshot --- each results file carries the run
timestamp its own citations were checked against, so a citation verified as
\texttt{FABRICATED} or \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE} reflects NVD's state at that specific time,
not a fixed point shared across every result in this paper. This asymmetry
(one path versioned and reproducible, the other live and time-varying) is a
disclosed property of the two authoritative sources' own APIs, not a
methodological inconsistency introduced by this pipeline --- restated in
Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion}'s limitations.

\subsection{Output guardrail: CVE-bait adversarial test}\label{subsec:cve-bait}

The CVE-bait set was expanded from an initial $n=6$ to $n=150$ real CVEs
across three passes (2026-08-12, 2026-08-12, 2026-08-25), the later
additions sourced in bulk from CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities
(KEV) catalog with each alert's behavior description paraphrased from
that CVE's own published description (vendor/product name and CVE
framing stripped, so the bait still tests spontaneous citation rather
than pattern-matching a restated name). Every one of the 150 numbers is
real and individually verified. 3 of the 150 alerts explicitly ask the
model to name an identifier it was not given; the remaining 147 are
purely symptom-only.

\textbf{Result} (\texttt{experiments/results/cve\_bait\_results.json}, $n=150$): 2/150
alerts (1.3\%, 95\% Wilson CI [0.4\%, 4.7\%]) produced an ungrounded CVE
citation --- a blended figure that conflates two conditions. \textbf{0/147
symptom-only alerts ever produced a spontaneous citation} (95\% CI [0.0\%,
2.6\%]); both hits come from the 3 explicit-ask alerts (2/3), once correct
and once with a real-but-wrong neighbor (below). The 0/147 figure is the
correct headline number for spontaneous hallucination rate.
\texttt{requires\_review} for this set (5/150, 3.3\%) is not the same as the
ungrounded rate --- 3 of the 5 are the PII guardrail firing on a
single-word product name (\texttt{Zimbra}, \texttt{Ray}, \texttt{Joomla}) misread as a person's
name (Sect.~\ref{subsec:pii-guardrail}, \ref{subsubsec:pii-bait}), not a grounding error; a metric-definition bug
conflating the two was found and fixed at this expansion.

Of the two ungrounded citations: the Log4Shell alert (\texttt{BAIT-002})
correctly produced \texttt{CVE-2021-44228}, classified \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE} ---
still flagged for review since the alert text never stated the number, a
policy artifact rather than an error. The Follina alert (\texttt{BAIT-017})
produced \texttt{CVE-2022-34713} (``DogWalk'') instead of the correct
\texttt{CVE-2022-30190} --- a real, separate Microsoft MSDT vulnerability from the
same disclosure window, classified \texttt{REAL\_BUT\_IRRELEVANT}. \textbf{This is a
concrete instance of exactly the risk this paper's introduction
describes}: a real identifier, confused with a closely related one,
indistinguishable from a correct citation without independent
verification. It is the only one of the 150 alerts where the model's
citation was actually \textit{wrong}, rather than correct-but-flagged.

\subsection{Output guardrail: ATT\&CK-bait adversarial test}\label{subsec:attack-bait}

A parallel bait set covers MITRE ATT\&CK technique citations, expanded
from $n=6$ to $n=150$ across two passes (2026-08-21, 2026-08-25). Every
technique paraphrases its real, official MITRE description into
symptom-only EDR/log-style telemetry, never stating the technique name or
ID, with an automated check confirming no ID-shaped token or the
technique's own name leaks into the alert text. 3 of the 150 alerts
explicitly request a technique ID, mirroring CVE-bait's ratio.

\textbf{Result} (\texttt{experiments/results/attack\_bait\_results.json}, $n=150$): 6/150
alerts (4.0\%, 95\% Wilson CI [1.8\%, 8.5\%]) produced an ungrounded ATT\&CK
citation. Of the 148 symptom-only alerts, \textbf{4/148 (2.7\%, 95\% CI [1.1\%,
6.7\%])} produced one; both explicit-ask alerts produced one (2/2). Of
the 6: 2 named the expected technique exactly (\texttt{ATTACK-BAIT-023},
\texttt{ATTACK-BAIT-081}, both \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE}). A new finding beyond
anything CVE-bait produced: \texttt{ATTACK-BAIT-005} cited \texttt{T1076}, a technique
ID that exists in the STIX snapshot but is marked \texttt{revoked} --- MITRE's own
record of a deprecated/superseded ID --- classified \texttt{REVOKED} rather than
\texttt{REAL\_BUT\_IRRELEVANT}: a real identifier that no longer represents an
active part of the framework, the ATT\&CK equivalent of citing a withdrawn
CVE. The remaining 3 (\texttt{ATTACK-BAIT-006}, \texttt{-078}, \texttt{-116}) are
\texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE}/\texttt{REAL\_BUT\_IRRELEVANT} wrong-neighbor citations in the
same shape as CVE-bait's Follina/DogWalk case --- \texttt{ATTACK-BAIT-006} is a
particularly close miss, citing sub-technique \texttt{T1542.003} when the
expected parent technique was \texttt{T1542}.

\textbf{What this shows:} the grounding-and-classify pattern generalizes to a
second, structurally different claim type (a static STIX snapshot rather
than a live per-ID API) --- the concrete evidence behind Contribution 2's
generalization claim. The \texttt{REVOKED} case also shows the taxonomy
(Sect.~\ref{subsec:taxonomy}) earning its keep on a case CVE-bait's own set never produced:
a real-but-deprecated identifier, materially different from either a
correct citation or an outright fabrication.

\subsection{The central empirical result: SelfCheckGPT versus deterministic grounding}\label{subsec:selfcheckgpt}

\textbf{Method.} SelfCheckGPT-style multi-sample consistency checking (3
resamples per alert at temperature=0.7 --- production runs at
temperature=0.1, Sect.~\ref{subsec:setup}, and would give resampling no diversity to
measure) against the same 60-alert CVE pool used in Sect.~\ref{subsec:realworld} (30
``prompted''/bait, CVE withheld; 30 ``stated'', CVE given). \texttt{sample\_citations()}
bypasses the guardrail pipeline entirely --- only the raw generator's
citation behavior across resamples is measured --- to make the
self-consistency-vs-external-grounding contrast from Sect.~\ref{sec:related-work} empirical
rather than only architectural.

\textbf{Result} (\texttt{experiments/results/selfcheckgpt\_results.json}, $n=60$; 4
alerts excluded where every resample declined to cite anything at all ---
nothing to score consistency on, 3 from the stated class, 1 from
prompted):

\begin{table}[h]
\caption{SelfCheckGPT self-consistency baseline: correctness by grounded/prompted class ($n=60$, 4 excluded)}\label{tab:selfcheckgpt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
Class & $n$ scored & SelfCheckGPT correct \\
\midrule
Stated (grounded) & 27 (3 declined) & 27/27 \\
Prompted (bait, withheld) & 29 (1 declined) & 9/29 (recall 0.31) \\
\botrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Overall on the 56 scored alerts: accuracy 0.643 (95\% CI [0.512, 0.755]),
precision 1.0 (95\% CI [0.701, 1.0]), recall 0.310 (95\% CI [0.173,
0.492]). SelfCheckGPT never false-flags a grounded citation as unstable,
matching Sect.~\ref{subsec:realworld}'s stated-class finding.

\textbf{The 20 missed prompted-class alerts are not what a bare recall number
suggests.} All 20 are cases where the model cited the \textit{same} CVE across
all 3 resamples --- perfectly self-consistent, so SelfCheckGPT correctly
reports no instability. But checking each majority citation against
ground truth shows \textbf{18 of the 20 are the correct identifier} --- the
model consistently recalled the real CVE from its own training knowledge,
even though it was withheld and never appears in the alert's evidence.
Only 2 of 20 are genuine misattributions, both citing \texttt{CVE-2021-31207} (a
real Exchange Server vulnerability from the same 2021 ProxyShell cluster)
in place of the alerts' actual ground truth (\texttt{CVE-2021-34473},
\texttt{CVE-2021-26855}).

\textbf{What this means.} SelfCheckGPT cannot distinguish ``consistently
grounded'' from ``consistently correct but ungrounded,'' because both look
identical to a consistency-only signal. Eighteen of these twenty cases
are, functionally, Real-and-Plausible citations (Sect.~\ref{subsec:taxonomy}) at a volume
this project's low-temperature (0.1) bait tests (Sects.~\ref{subsec:cve-bait},~\ref{subsec:realworld}) never
observed --- those recorded exactly one such case across their combined
$n=106$. Raising sampling temperature to what SelfCheckGPT requires makes
the model volunteer a withheld-but-correct identifier far more often than
production settings do --- a boundary condition on Sect.~\ref{subsec:realworld}'s ``never
volunteers when withheld'' finding, which holds at temperature=0.1
specifically, not as a general property of the model. The deterministic
grounding checker still correctly flags every one of these 20 as
ungrounded, since groundedness is about evidence presence, not
real-world correctness --- the distinction Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion}'s central argument is
built on. The two misattribution cases echo Sect.~\ref{subsec:cve-bait}'s Follina/DogWalk
finding at smaller scale: a real identifier from the right cluster, but
the wrong specific one.

\subsection{Significance testing: deterministic grounding versus SelfCheckGPT}\label{subsec:mcnemar}

A paired McNemar test between the deterministic pipeline and SelfCheckGPT
needed a separate run rather than reusing Sect.~\ref{subsec:selfcheckgpt}'s data:
\texttt{sample\_citations()} bypasses the guardrail pipeline, so the
deterministic checker's actual verdict was never computed on those 60
alerts --- only the bait/stated construction's ground-truth label was, and
treating that label as a stand-in for the checker's verdict would make
the comparison tautological. Generated one fresh report per alert (not
three --- the deterministic checker needs no resampling) at the same
temperature=0.7, over the identical 60-alert set, and ran the real
deterministic checker against each
(\texttt{experiments/evaluation/selfcheckgpt\_significance\_test.py}).

\textbf{Result} (\texttt{experiments/results/selfcheckgpt\_vs\_deterministic\_mcnemar.json},
$n=56$ after excluding the same declined-every-sample alerts Sect.~\ref{subsec:selfcheckgpt}
excludes): \textbf{both correct in 32 cases, the deterministic checker uniquely
correct in 16, SelfCheckGPT uniquely correct in 4, both wrong in 4 ---
exact binomial McNemar $p=0.0118$, significant at $\alpha=0.05$.} This is the one
comparison in this paper where a raw performance difference is both large
and statistically confirmed rather than resting on argument alone: the
deterministic checker was right $4\times$ more often than SelfCheckGPT on the
exact same alerts. The deterministic checker's own accuracy across all 60
was 52/60 (100\% on the stated class, 73.3\% on the prompted class).

Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:llmjudge}'s LLM-judge result showed zero disagreement with the
deterministic pipeline (100\% both ways), which --- like Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:inputguardrailcomparison}'s
degenerate LLM-Guard-vs-hybrid trial --- would itself produce a degenerate,
zero-discordant-pair McNemar test rather than a meaningful $p$-value, so it
was not run.

\subsection{Relevance classifier validation}\label{subsec:relevance}

\textbf{Method.} The \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE}/\texttt{REAL\_BUT\_IRRELEVANT} split (Sect.~\ref{subsec:taxonomy})
rests on Sect.~\ref{subsec:output-guardrail}'s Stage 2 relevance score --- a deterministic, stemmed
bag-of-words overlap between the alert's evidence text and the
authoritative record's description, thresholded at 0.15 --- which had
never been checked against independent judgment before this evaluation.

80 (alert, candidate CVE) pairs were built from the CVE-bait set
(Sect.~\ref{subsec:cve-bait}): 40 anchor alerts, each paired with its own correct CVE and a
different real CVE from the same pool by a fixed index shift. Real NVD
descriptions were fetched live for all 80 candidates --- the same text
Sect.~\ref{subsec:output-guardrail}'s Stage 2 actually scores. Each pair was labeled
relevant/not-relevant by a human rater, blind to which CVE was intended
as correct and blind to row order. An AI-suggested first pass (0/80 rater
overrides) was followed by a fully blind re-check of the 3 pairs flagged
as genuinely ambiguous; all 3 held, including the two closest calls ---
disclosed as AI-suggested, human-confirmed labeling, not fully
independent from-scratch annotation.

\textbf{Result} (\texttt{experiments/evaluation/relevance\_classifier\_validation/},
$n=80$): \textbf{accuracy 92.5\% (95\% CI [84.6\%, 96.5\%]), precision 90.5\% (95\% CI
[77.9\%, 96.2\%]), recall 95.0\% (95\% CI [83.5\%, 98.6\%]), F1 92.7\%}
(TP=38, FP=4, TN=36, FN=2). All 6 disagreements cluster tightly around
the 0.15 decision threshold (overlap scores 0.100--0.191) rather than
spreading across the full range --- a boundary-case failure mode, not
general unreliability. One genuinely correct citation scored only 0.100
and was misclassified as irrelevant because its official NVD description
is just the bare title ``Windows Print Spooler Elevation of Privilege
Vulnerability,'' almost no text for a word-overlap scorer to match
against; a second correct citation missed the threshold by one hundredth
of a point.

\textbf{What this means.} The relevance classifier is reasonably accurate
against human judgment (92.5\%), which is what makes the
\texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE}/\texttt{REAL\_BUT\_IRRELEVANT} split defensible rather than
resting on an unchecked heuristic. Its concrete failure mode --- short or
generic authoritative descriptions starving a bag-of-words scorer ---
produces false negatives on genuinely correct citations, a disclosed
limitation restated in Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Real-world validation: third-party incident generator and CVE pool}\label{subsec:realworld}

To move beyond hand-authored bait alerts, \texttt{Secure\_SOC\_AI} \cite{securesocai} (an
independent, third-party open-source SOC tool) was integrated as an
incident \textit{generator} only --- its own triage step was not used, since
replacing exactly that step is this project's contribution. Its rule
engine and correlator produced 76 incidents across all 7 of its shipped
detection rules, run through the full LLMCite pipeline.

\textbf{Result:} 0\% ungrounded on both CVE and ATT\&CK citations, 0\% requiring
review across all 76 incidents --- an expected clean result on
non-adversarial, rule-engine-generated incidents, included for
completeness rather than as an adversarial stress test (Sects.~\ref{subsec:cve-bait}--\ref{subsec:attack-bait}
cover that).

A second, purpose-built 60-alert CVE pool (15 real NVD-listed CVEs, split
into \textbf{bait} style --- exploit behavior described, CVE withheld --- and
\textbf{stated} style --- CVE given directly) isolates whether the model can
\textit{identify} a CVE from behavior alone, or only \textit{verify} one it is already
given.

\textbf{This is the most important finding in this subsection.} The model never

capability gap motivating specific future work (retrieval-based CVE
matching against behavioral description), not a guardrail failure. \textbf{This
finding holds at temperature=0.1; Sect.~\ref{subsec:selfcheckgpt} finds the opposite behavior
at temperature=0.7} on the same style of alert.

\subsection{Live integration demonstration: real Wazuh SIEM data}\label{subsec:wazuh}

To test against genuinely live, non-synthetic alert data rather than only
CICIDS2017 \cite{cicids2017} (flagged elsewhere as outdated for current attack-pattern
coverage --- see Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion}), a local Wazuh \cite{wazuh} SIEM/XDR stack was deployed via
Docker Compose with a real registered agent, generating real triggering
activity across five trigger types: File Integrity Monitoring on an
actual file write; a genuine SSH brute-force attempt that Wazuh's
correlation engine correctly escalated to a dedicated rule; realistic
SQLi/XSS/directory-traversal request lines against Wazuh's web-attack
ruleset; rootkit-signature markers via Wazuh's rootcheck module; and
sudo/privilege-escalation syslog activity against Wazuh's sudo ruleset.

\textbf{Result:} 139 unique alerts (deduplicated, expanded from an original
26) run through the full pipeline: \textbf{0\% ungrounded on ATT\&CK/CVE across
all 139}. Manual inspection confirmed the model's reasoning engaged with
real MITRE technique IDs present in the alerts (\textbf{T1110} Brute Force on
SSH alerts; \textbf{T1190} Exploit Public-Facing Application on web-attack
alerts; \textbf{T1548.003} Sudo and Sudo Caching on privilege-escalation
alerts), once an adapter bug stripping MITRE tags from SSH-rule alerts
specifically was found and fixed.

\textbf{Two genuine PII-guardrail bugs surfaced by this live data, neither
visible on synthetic benchmarks.} First, at $n=26$, 5 alerts triggered a
\texttt{PERSON} false positive --- the NER model misread technical strings (a URL
path, a shell-command fragment, ``ATT\&CK'', a document name) as a person's
name. Fixed with a plausibility filter rejecting matches containing
characters no real name uses. Second, the bulk expansion to $n=139$ raised
\texttt{requires\_review} to 27.3\% (38/139) before correction --- $\sim$35 of the 38
were Presidio's \texttt{PHONE\_NUMBER} recognizer misreading bare IP addresses;
not threshold-fixable, since a real IP and a real phone number score
identically, so fixed with a structural check rejecting any
\texttt{PHONE\_NUMBER} match that parses as a valid IPv4 address. The corrected
result is \textbf{\texttt{requires\_review} at 4/139 (2.9\%)}, with zero regression on
real-name or real-phone-number detection. The 4 remaining flags are
single capitalized words (``Mithra'', ``Maniac'', ``Bash'', ``Benchmark'') the
model's own report text uses as a rootkit/benchmark name beside
``rootkit'' or ``CIS \ldots{} score'' --- genuinely ambiguous for the NER model,
reported as a known limitation rather than overfit to four exact strings.

\textbf{Honest limitation:} $n=139$ is manual, burst-fired activity across
several sessions rather than continuous production traffic --- sufficient
to demonstrate the pipeline against many real, live-detected attack
patterns and to surface two real guardrail bugs, not sufficient for a
precise statistical claim on its own. Wazuh's built-in
vulnerability-detector (which would add genuine CVE-tagged alerts) was
blocked by a single-node Docker inventory-sync limitation and is not
counted among the 139.

\subsection{Supporting evaluation: auxiliary guardrails and engineering baselines}\label{subsec:supporting}

Sects.~\ref{subsec:cve-bait}--\ref{subsec:crosssource} above establish and validate this paper's central claim:
the CVE/ATT\&CK grounding-and-classify pipeline works across two claim
types, and the \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE} class it introduces catches a real,
statistically confirmed blind spot in the leading self-consistency
alternative. The four subsections below are reported for completeness ---
they validate the pipeline's other guardrail layers (input filtering, PII
redaction), an alternative verification strategy (LLM-as-judge), and
engineering-level throughput characteristics --- but none of them bear
directly on the paper's central claim, and none should be read as
carrying equal evidentiary weight to Sects.~\ref{subsec:cve-bait}--\ref{subsec:crosssource} above.

\subsubsection{LLM-judge baseline}\label{subsubsec:llmjudge}

As an alternative verification strategy, a judge call is prompted
directly with an (alert evidence, generated report) pair and asked to
classify citations as grounded/ungrounded, without the deterministic
pipeline's verdict --- run over a class-balanced calibration set with two
judges: \texttt{openai/gpt-oss-20b} (same-family) and \texttt{qwen/qwen3.6-27b} (a
genuinely different, independently-hosted family, addressing Zheng et
al.'s \cite{llmjudge2023} self-enhancement-bias concern directly). Both reproduced the
deterministic pipeline's labels exactly: \textbf{100\% accuracy/precision/recall}
on the same-family run ($n=318$, complete, 95\% CI [98.8\%, 100\%]) and the
cross-family run ($n=450$, \textbf{442/450 (98\%) completed}, 95\% CI [99.1\%,
100\%] on 440 scored samples). \textbf{The two sets are not sample-for-sample
identical} --- the underlying bait-alert pool grew between runs, so the
same-family 318 are a strict subset of the cross-family 450 (tracked in
\texttt{docs/ROADMAP\_PLAN.md} \S14); on every shared sample the judges agree
100\%.

\textbf{This result is weaker evidence than the headline number suggests.} The
harder tier's discriminating signal is close to what the deterministic
checker already computes --- an identifier-shaped token appearing in the
evidence --- so a capable judge matching it is an expected feasibility
floor, not deep semantic reasoning. The defensible claim is narrower: an
LLM judge can reproduce this binary decision regardless of model family,
while the deterministic pipeline keeps real deployment advantages neither
judge shares --- near-zero latency, no per-call cost or rate-limit exposure,
no dependence on a hosted API.

\subsubsection{PII redaction guardrail: bait test (Threat T3)}\label{subsubsec:pii-bait}

Unlike Sects.~\ref{subsec:cve-bait}--\ref{subsec:attack-bait}'s grounding checks, T3 is a \textit{redaction} threat: does
the report echo sensitive data present in the raw alert. The guardrail
(Sect.~\ref{subsec:pii-guardrail}) runs Presidio \cite{presidio2023} and spaCy's \texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm} locally.

\texttt{experiments/evaluation/pii\_bait\_alerts.py} built 40 alerts with
synthetic PII across 10 scenario types (DLP exfiltration, phishing
credential harvest, payment-data exposure, database dump, cloud-storage
misconfiguration, and others), plus 20 clean alerts as a false-positive
check. Beyond the original 6 PII/8 clean hand-authored alerts, the
expansion embeds real synthetic entity values --- never real individuals'
data --- from two verified, actively-maintained datasets (Gretel's
\texttt{gretel-pii-masking-en-v1}, ai4privacy's \texttt{pii-masking-openpii-1m})
wrapped in hand-written scenarios, each verified via the guardrail's own
detector to actually trigger its stated entity type before inclusion.

\textbf{Result} (\texttt{experiments/results/pii\_bait\_results.json}, $n=60$): \textbf{5/40
PII alerts had a genuine detection (12.5\%, 95\% CI [5.5\%, 26.1\%]), 0/20
false positives, 0 residual PII after redaction} --- consistent with the
original $n=6$ rate (16.7\%), now with a real confidence interval. All 5
detections caught only one of the alert's two expected entity types,
never both; manual inspection of ``nothing detected'' cases confirms the
model (\texttt{openai/gpt-oss-20b}) consistently summarizes PII \textit{abstractly}
rather than quoting raw values.

Two of the initial 7 raw detections were false positives --- the NER model
flagged the bare word ``PII'' and the phrase ``enforce bucket'' as \texttt{PERSON}
--- caught by manually checking every detection against its known sourced
value rather than trusting the aggregate rate. Fixed by extending the
plausibility filter (real names are Title Case; reject lowercase-starting
or acronym-shaped spans), correcting the rate from an inflated 17.5\%
(7/40) to the verified 12.5\% (5/40) above.

\textbf{What this means.} The model's own summarization behavior is itself a
meaningful T3 mitigation; on the cases where it did quote something
verbatim, the guardrail worked end to end with zero residual PII and
zero false positives elsewhere. And checking a guardrail's \textit{aggregate
rate} is not the same as checking that every individual hit is genuine ---
a type-level pass/fail check can silently absorb false positives that
happen to land on the right label.

\subsubsection{Concurrency and throughput benchmarks}\label{subsubsec:concurrency}

An original benchmark (1/2/4 workers, 3 repeats looped inside one
long-running process) showed threading's full-pipeline throughput
improving from 1 to 2 workers but collapsing sharply at 4 (0.67
alerts/sec), consistent with (not definitively proven to be) Groq
server-side per-key throttling under load; multiprocessing was worse
than threading at every worker count.

This was \textbf{redone 2026-08-25} to close two gaps: repeats looping
inside one long-running process can let OS scheduler/allocator state
carry over between them, and there was no way to separate guardrail
overhead from live Groq network variance. Fixes: (1) each repeat runs as
a genuinely independent subprocess (\texttt{fresh\_process\_benchmark.py});
(2) a mocked-LLM variant runs the real guardrail/grounding/redaction
pipeline but replaces only the Groq call with a fixed 0.3s delay, free of
API cost and rate limits, so it runs at $n=30$ instead of $n=6$.

Fresh-process isolation surfaced a real bug: full-pipeline runs with more
than one thread crashed on an unlocked lazy-singleton race in the
pytector loader (two threads racing on the first concurrent call both
started constructing the DeBERTa model at once) --- a real production
risk, not just a benchmark artifact, fixed with double-checked locking.

reproduce the original benchmark's sharp 4-thread collapse --- 4-thread
throughput here is the \textit{best} of the three worker counts, not the worst.
Both runs are $n=6$, modest statistical power either way; this could be a
real but non-reproduced throttling instance, or cross-run interference in
the original in-process design (e.g.\ rate-limit-window state persisting
across the sequential 1$\to$2$\to$4 tests). Neither explanation is confirmed ---
both results are reported rather than one quietly replacing the other.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE} means for trust.} The central practical argument
of this paper is that a citation which is real and topically plausible is
the \textit{hardest} case for a human reviewer to catch, not the easiest --- it looks
correct. Sect.~\ref{subsec:taxonomy}'s classification taxonomy and its unconditional review-flag
policy exist specifically because an earlier version of this pipeline got
this wrong (treating \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE} as self-evidently safe), and the
fix is documented as a deliberate correction, not presented as though the
pipeline always worked this way. Sects.~\ref{subsec:selfcheckgpt}--\ref{subsec:mcnemar} make this more than an
architectural argument: a leading alternative (self-consistency checking)
is demonstrably fooled by this exact pattern, and the gap is large enough
to be statistically confirmed. Everything reported after Sect.~\ref{subsec:relevance} in this
paper's evaluation exists to establish that the mechanism producing this
result actually works across real, third-party, and live data --- it
supports this finding, it is not a separate finding of equal weight.

\textbf{What the CVE-pool finding (Sect.~\ref{subsec:realworld}) means.} The 0\%-fabrication /
0\%-spontaneous-citation result is genuinely two findings, not one: the
pipeline's grounding check is not the reason the model stays silent (there
is nothing to ground-check if nothing is cited) --- the model itself,
independent of any guardrail, does not volunteer technical identifiers it
wasn't given. This is safe behavior, but it also means the current system
architecturally cannot help an analyst who needs a CVE \textit{identified} from
symptoms, only one \textit{verified} once cited. We name this explicitly as the
boundary of the current contribution rather than implying broader coverage
than what was tested.

\textbf{Limitations, stated plainly:}
\begin{itemize}
\item Sects.~\ref{subsec:selfcheckgpt}--\ref{subsec:mcnemar} (SelfCheckGPT and its McNemar test) are both
  complete: the deterministic checker is significantly more accurate
  ($p=0.0118$, $n=56$). Its result also contradicts Sect.~\ref{subsec:realworld}'s ``never
  volunteers a withheld CVE'' finding at higher sampling temperature (0.7
  vs.\ 0.1) --- the same model volunteers a withheld-but-correct
  identifier once sampled at the temperature SelfCheckGPT requires, so
  Sect.~\ref{subsec:realworld}'s finding should be read as holding at production
  temperature specifically, not as a general model property.
  Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:llmjudge}'s LLM-judge baseline now spans same- and
  cross-model-family judges --- 442/450 (98\%) complete, quota-gated on
  Groq's free tier rather than a design limitation.
\item Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:llmjudge}'s 100\% LLM-judge agreement should not be read as
  strong evidence on its own: the hard tier's discriminating signal is
  close to what the deterministic checker already computes, a
  feasibility floor rather than deep semantic reasoning, not evidence
  against the deterministic pipeline's necessity.
\item The CVE-bait result (Sect.~\ref{subsec:cve-bait}, $n=150$) gives a citable 95\% CI
  ([0.4\%, 4.7\%]), but both ungrounded citations occurred on the same
  two famous vulnerabilities (Log4Shell, Follina) --- the honest claim is
  ``rarely induces spontaneous citation, catches it on famous cases,''
  not ``stress-tested against obscure-CVE hallucination.'' The
  ATT\&CK-bait result (Sect.~\ref{subsec:attack-bait}) differs: 4 of its 6 ungrounded
  citations are on less prominent, wrong-neighbor or revoked-technique
  cases.
\item Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:concurrency}'s concurrency benchmark uses fresh-process repeats; the
  input-guardrail latency comparison (Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:inputguardrailcomparison}) has not had the
  same treatment, so only order-of-magnitude comparisons should be drawn
  from it. The Wazuh live demonstration (Sect.~\ref{subsec:wazuh}, $n=139$) is a
  real-world sanity check and bug-finding exercise, not a statistically
  powered claim.
\item The ATT\&CK verification path relies on a periodically refreshed local
  snapshot (Sect.~\ref{subsec:setup}) rather than a live per-ID lookup (none exists
  publicly) --- a disclosed asymmetry with the CVE path's live NVD
  lookups. CICIDS2017, used for benign-traffic text in
  Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:inputguardrailcomparison}, is independently flagged as no longer
  representative of current attack patterns \cite{goldschmidtchuda2025};
  used here only for \texttt{BENIGN}-labeled text, while the live Wazuh
  integration (Sect.~\ref{subsec:wazuh}) is relied on for any current-attack-data
  claim.
\item T3 (PII leakage, Sect.~\ref{subsec:pii-guardrail}/\ref{subsubsec:pii-bait}) is at $n=60$: 5/40 detected
  (12.5\%, 95\% CI [5.5\%, 26.1\%]), corrected down from an initial 7/40
  after 2 false positives were caught by checking sourced values rather
  than trusting the type-level aggregate.
\item The report-generation backend is a single small model
  (\texttt{openai/gpt-oss-20b} via Groq); generalization to larger or
  differently-trained models is untested.
\item The relevance classifier behind \texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE}/\texttt{REAL\_BUT\_IRRELEVANT}
  (Sect.~\ref{subsec:output-guardrail}, validated Sect.~\ref{subsec:relevance}) is 92.5\% accurate against human
  judgment ($n=80$), not perfect --- its 6 disagreements cluster at the
  0.15 decision threshold, with a known failure mode on short/generic
  authoritative descriptions. Its labels are AI-suggested and
  human-confirmed, not independently annotated from scratch.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Threats to validity.} The input-guardrail comparison (Sect.~\ref{subsubsec:inputguardrailcomparison})
uses a dataset partly adapted from public sources rather than fully
independent attacker data, tracked per-sample via a \texttt{provenance} field
so this can be audited. The CVE-bait set (Sect.~\ref{subsec:cve-bait}, $n=150$) is not a
random sample of all CVEs --- it is biased toward well-documented,
high-profile, actively exploited vulnerabilities (25 individually chosen
for fame, 125 from CISA's KEV catalog), a deliberate choice (an obscure
CVE with no public writeup is not a meaningful spontaneous-citation test
either way) that means the estimated rate should not be read as
generalizing to arbitrary, less-documented CVEs. The ATT\&CK-bait set
(Sect.~\ref{subsec:attack-bait}, $n=150$) has the analogous bias in the other direction ---
technique selection is drawn from the local MITRE snapshot rather than
any real-world prevalence signal, so its rate reflects how often the
model reaches for a specific technique ID given a resembling symptom
description, not attacker behavior frequency.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented LLMCite, a domain-specialized citation-grounding verification
pipeline for LLM-generated SOC reports, built around a four-class outcome
taxonomy (plus a fifth label for formally withdrawn identifiers) rather
than a binary flagged/not-flagged label. The taxonomy's central claim ---
that a real, topically plausible citation never actually stated in the
evidence (\texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE}) is the hardest case for a human reviewer to
catch, precisely because it looks correct --- is not argued architecturally
alone. Sects.~\ref{subsec:selfcheckgpt}--\ref{subsec:mcnemar} demonstrate it directly: at the sampling temperature
self-consistency checking requires, the model volunteers real,
correct-but-uncited identifiers it stays silent on at production
temperature (Sect.~\ref{subsec:realworld}), and does so consistently enough, across
independent resamples, that a SelfCheckGPT-style self-consistency signal
alone cannot tell eighteen of these cases apart from a genuinely grounded
citation. External grounding can, because it checks the evidence the model
was actually given rather than the model's confidence in its own answer ---
and the resulting accuracy gap between the two approaches is large enough
to be statistically confirmed on the identical alerts (McNemar $p=0.0118$),
not merely observed. That distinction --- not raw accuracy against a
baseline --- is what this paper argues a citation-grounding pipeline
contributes that self-consistency checking alone does not, and it is the
one comparison in this paper we would ask a reader to remember above the
rest.

That finding rests on a pipeline validated broadly, not narrowly, though
the breadth below is supporting evidence for the claim above, not a set
of separate contributions carrying equal weight. The grounding-and-classify
pattern generalizes across two distinct claim types (CVE identifiers via
live NVD lookup, MITRE ATT\&CK techniques via a local STIX snapshot),
evaluated via adversarial bait tests at $n=150$ for each claim type, an
independent third-party incident generator, a CVE pool isolating
identification from verification, live SIEM data including a genuinely
detected attack, and a pooled cross-source grounding summary spanning 575
alerts across every evaluated source (Sect.~\ref{subsec:crosssource}). Supporting evaluation of
the pipeline's other guardrail layers --- a statistically tested comparison
of the input-guardrail layer against maintained open-source alternatives,
an LLM-judge baseline now spanning both a same-family and a cross-family
judge (the latter 442/450, 98\% complete and quota-gated rather than left
as an unaddressed limitation), and a bait-tested PII-redaction guardrail
($n=60$, 12.5\% detection rate, 95\% CI [5.5\%, 26.1\%]) --- further validates the
system without altering the central finding above.
