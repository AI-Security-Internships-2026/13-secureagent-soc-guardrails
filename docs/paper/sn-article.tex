\abstract{Large language models are increasingly used to generate Security Operations Center (SOC) threat reports, automatically summarizing alerts and citing supporting evidence such as CVE identifiers and MITRE ATT\&CK techniques. These citations are rarely verified: models — particularly smaller, low-latency variants favored for real-time triage — can fabricate plausible-sounding identifiers, misattribute real ones to the wrong context, or recall genuinely correct citations the source alert never actually provided. A flat "flagged/not flagged" treatment of these cases obscures an important distinction: a wholly invented CVE and a real-but-unevidenced one carry different risk profiles for an analyst deciding how much to trust the report.

We present LLMCite, a two-stage groundedness-verification pipeline for LLM-generated SOC reports. Stage one deterministically checks whether a cited CVE or ATT&CK identifier is supported by the source alert; stage two verifies ungrounded citations against authoritative external sources (the National Vulnerability Database and the MITRE ATT&CK STIX corpus) and classifies each into one of four categories — Fabricated, Real-but-Irrelevant, Real-and-Plausible, or Unverified — rather than a binary label. We show that Real-and-Plausible citations, despite superficially appearing legitimate, represent the case most likely to evade existing review triggers, and evaluate SecCite against an adversarial CVE/ATT&CK-citation benchmark and [N] baseline detectors, including an LLM-as-judge comparator. [Headline result once measured, e.g., "LLMCite achieves X\% precision/recall at Yms latency, outperforming..."]. We position SecCite as a domain-specialized instance of citation-grounding verification for security-critical LLM applications, distinct from prior citation-hallucination work targeting scholarly references}

\subsection{Paper 1 --- InjecAgent \cite{injecagent2024}}\label{subsec:litreview-p1}

\begin{description}
\item[Full title] InjecAgent: Benchmarking Indirect Prompt Injections in Tool-Integrated Large Language Model Agents
\item[Authors] Zhan, Q., Liang, Z., Ying, Z., Kang, D.
\item[Year] 2024
\item[Venue] Findings of ACL 2024
\item[URL/DOI] \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02691}
\item[Method] Benchmark of 1{,}054 test cases evaluating tool-integrated LLM agents against indirect prompt injection embedded in tool outputs (e.g.\ emails, web content, logs)
\item[Dataset] Synthetic tool-use scenarios across multiple agent tasks
\item[Key result] Reinforcing attacker instructions with a ``hacking prompt'' nearly doubles the attack success rate against ReAct-prompted GPT-4
\item[Limitation] Synthetic benchmark; does not cover real-world SOC log formats or domain-specific injection patterns
\item[Relevance] Directly models the threat in this project's input guardrail layer --- malicious instructions hidden inside alert/log data that the SOC agent ingests (maps to RQ3)
\end{description}

\medskip\noindent\textit{Notes:} Most directly applicable paper for the ``Input Guardrail'' box in the architecture diagram. Useful as a template for building our own injection benchmark in \texttt{experiments/}.

\subsection{Paper 2 --- SelfCheckGPT \cite{selfcheckgpt2023}}\label{subsec:litreview-p2}

\begin{description}
\item[Full title] SelfCheckGPT: Zero-Resource Black-Box Hallucination Detection for Generative Large Language Models
\item[Authors] Manakul, P., Liusie, A., Gales, M.
\item[Year] 2023
\item[Venue] EMNLP 2023
\item[URL/DOI] \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08896}
\item[Method] Samples multiple stochastic responses to the same prompt and measures consistency between them --- sentences that vary a lot across samples are flagged as likely hallucinated, with no external knowledge base required
\item[Dataset] WikiBio-based passages generated by GPT-3, human-annotated for factuality at the sentence level
\item[Key result] Sentence-level consistency scoring detects non-factual sentences competitively with methods that require access to the model's token-level output probabilities, despite being fully black-box
\item[Limitation] Zero-resource means it can't confirm a claim is \textit{true} against a ground truth --- only that the model is \textit{inconsistent} about it, which is a weaker (but cheaper) signal than external verification
\item[Relevance] Direct methodological anchor for the project's planned SelfCheckGPT comparison (Aug 23 milestone) --- contrasts with this project's own approach, which verifies against an authoritative external source (NVD/MITRE) rather than checking self-consistency alone
\end{description}

\medskip\noindent\textit{Notes:} Replaces the earlier ``Survey on Hallucination in LLMs'' citation --- that survey's own most useful content \textit{was} a description of this paper, so citing SelfCheckGPT directly is strictly better: it's the primary source, and it's the actual technique in scope for the cross-method comparison later in the project.

\subsection{Paper 3 --- TRAM (Threat Report ATT\&CK Mapper) \cite{tram2023}}\label{subsec:litreview-p3}

\begin{description}
\item[Full title] Threat Report ATT\&CK Mapper (TRAM)
\item[Authors] MITRE Engenuity Center for Threat-Informed Defense (with CrowdStrike as a contributing partner on the LLM-based version)
\item[Year] v1: 2021; LLM-based update: 2023
\item[Venue] Open-source project (GitHub, Center for Threat-Informed Defense)
\item[URL/DOI] \url{https://github.com/mitre-attack/tram} ; \url{https://ctid.mitre.org/projects/threat-report-attck-mapper-tram-v1/}
\item[Method] Trains/fine-tunes ML models (originally classical NLP, later LLM-based) to automatically identify MITRE ATT\&CK technique mentions in prose-format cyber threat intelligence reports, with a human-in-the-loop validation UI
\item[Dataset] Corpus of analyst-annotated threat intelligence reports mapped to ATT\&CK techniques
\item[Key result] Automates a task that is otherwise manual, slow, and inconsistent across analysts --- mapping free-text threat reports to structured ATT\&CK technique IDs
\item[Limitation] Extracts/maps technique mentions from \textit{trusted} analyst-authored reports; does not address the adversarial case this project targets --- an LLM \textit{fabricating} a technique ID that was never actually present, or citing a real-but-irrelevant one
\item[Relevance] Closest existing prior art to this project's MITRE ATT\&CK grounding checker (Week 9). TRAM solves ``find real technique mentions in text''; this project solves the complementary, adversarial problem --- ``verify a \textit{claimed} technique citation is real, current, and topically grounded'' --- worth stating explicitly as the point of differentiation
\end{description}

\medskip\noindent\textit{Notes:} Replaces the earlier ``AI-Augmented SOC Survey'' citation. This is a much stronger match --- it's the one existing system doing ATT\&CK-from-text extraction, which is exactly the space this project's own contribution sits in. Frame the comparison as extraction (TRAM) vs.\ adversarial verification (this project) --- that contrast is a good sentence for the paper's related-work gap statement.

\subsection{Paper 4 --- NeMo Guardrails \cite{nemoguardrails2023}}\label{subsec:litreview-p4}

\begin{description}
\item[Full title] NeMo Guardrails: A Toolkit for Controllable and Safe LLM Applications with Programmable Rails
\item[Authors] Rebedea, T., Dinu, R., et al.
\item[Year] 2023
\item[Venue] NVIDIA (toolkit paper)
\item[URL/DOI] \url{https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/NeMo-Guardrails}
\item[Method] Introduces Colang, a domain-specific language for defining programmable guardrail policies that intercept/validate input and output across five pipeline stages
\item[Dataset] N/A (framework)
\item[Key result] GPU-accelerated architecture achieves sub-100ms response times; native integration with LangChain/LangGraph
\item[Limitation] General-purpose conversational control; does not enforce provenance-aware authority ordering between system instructions and untrusted content unless explicitly configured
\item[Relevance] Evaluated directly in Week 2 as a candidate input-guardrail framework; its LLM-based Colang intent classification proved unreliable on a local 7B model for this project's injection-detection task, motivating the switch to a deterministic Python guardrail (documented as RQ5 in the proposal)
\end{description}

\medskip\noindent\textit{Notes:} Limitation noted here matters a lot for the SOC use case --- by default it won't distinguish ``trusted system prompt'' from ``untrusted log content'' without explicit configuration. See Paper 7 (Instruction Hierarchy) for a primary paper addressing exactly this gap.

\subsection{Paper 5 --- Presidio \cite{presidio2023}}\label{subsec:litreview-p5}

\begin{description}
\item[Full title] Presidio: An open-source framework for detecting, redacting, masking, and anonymizing sensitive data (PII)
\item[Authors] Microsoft
\item[Year] Ongoing (actively maintained)
\item[Venue] GitHub / Microsoft open source
\item[URL/DOI] \url{https://github.com/microsoft/presidio}
\item[Method] Hybrid PII detection combining Named Entity Recognition (spaCy), regex pattern matching, rule-based logic, and checksum validation
\item[Dataset] N/A (tool; ships with predefined recognizers for common entity types)
\item[Key result] Detects entities like names, emails, phone numbers, SSNs, credit cards, and locations, with configurable anonymization operators (replace, mask, hash, redact, encrypt)
\item[Limitation] Self-documented: automated detection provides no guarantee of catching all sensitive information; additional layered protections are recommended
\item[Relevance] Direct implementation choice for the output guardrail's PII redaction component; its stated limitation directly informs the ``PII leakage rate'' evaluation metric
\end{description}

\medskip\noindent\textit{Notes:} The explicit ``no guarantee'' caveat from Microsoft is worth quoting in the final report when discussing residual risk after guardrails are applied.

\subsection{Paper 6 --- FActScore \cite{factscore2023}}\label{subsec:litreview-p6}

\begin{description}
\item[Full title] FActScore: Fine-grained Atomic Evaluation of Factual Precision in Long Form Text Generation
\item[Authors] Min, S., Krishna, K., Lyu, X., Lewis, M., Yih, W., Koh, P.W., Iyyer, M., Zettlemoyer, L., Hajishirzi, H.
\item[Year] 2023
\item[Venue] EMNLP 2023
\item[URL/DOI] \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251}
\item[Method] Breaks a long-form generation into individual ``atomic facts,'' then checks each one against a reliable knowledge source, reporting the percentage supported
\item[Dataset] Human-written biographies generated by InstructGPT, ChatGPT, and PerplexityAI, with human factuality annotations; also an automated estimator validated against 6{,}500 generations from 13 LMs
\item[Key result] Even strong commercial models are far from perfectly factual (ChatGPT: 58\% atomic-fact precision on biographies); the automated FActScore estimator matches human judgment with under 2\% error
\item[Limitation] Designed for open-domain biographical text with a broad reference corpus (Wikipedia); doesn't address a domain where the ``knowledge source'' is a structured, versioned technical database (like NVD or MITRE ATT\&CK) rather than free-text biography articles
\item[Relevance] This project's core method --- decompose an LLM report into claims (CVE IDs, ATT\&CK technique IDs), verify each atomically against an authoritative source, classify by outcome --- is structurally the same pattern FActScore establishes, specialized to the security domain. Good citation for justifying the methodology, not just the SOC application
\end{description}

\medskip\noindent\textit{Notes:} This is the strongest ``prior art for our core method'' citation in the whole review --- worth citing prominently in the methodology section, not just related work, since the four-class taxonomy (\texttt{FABRICATED}/\texttt{REAL\_BUT\_IRRELEVANT}/\texttt{REAL\_AND\_PLAUSIBLE}/\texttt{UNVERIFIED}) is a domain-specialized descendant of this atomic-verification idea.

\subsection{Paper 7 --- The Instruction Hierarchy \cite{instructionhierarchy2024}}\label{subsec:litreview-p7}

\begin{description}
\item[Full title] The Instruction Hierarchy: Training LLMs to Prioritize Privileged Instructions
\item[Authors] Wallace, E., Xiao, K., Leike, R., Weng, L., Heidecke, J., Beutel, A.
\item[Year] 2024
\item[Venue] arXiv (OpenAI)
\item[URL/DOI] \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13208}
\item[Method] Proposes an explicit instruction hierarchy (system prompt \textgreater\ trusted user input \textgreater\ untrusted third-party content) and an automated synthetic-data generation method to fine-tune models to selectively ignore lower-privileged instructions when they conflict with higher-privileged ones
\item[Dataset] Synthetically generated conflicting-instruction examples; evaluated against held-out prompt injection and jailbreak attack types not seen during training
\item[Key result] Substantially increases robustness against injection/jailbreak attacks, including attack types absent from training data, with minimal loss of general capability
\item[Limitation] Requires fine-tuning access to the base model --- not applicable as a drop-in guardrail for a third-party API model (like this project's Groq-hosted Llama backend), where the defense has to live in the input/output layer instead
\item[Relevance] Fills the gap left by the InjecAgent paper (attack-side only) --- this is a primary paper on the \textit{defense} side, and it names precisely the property NeMo Guardrails' own documentation admits it lacks by default (see Paper 4's limitation): provenance-aware authority ordering between trusted and untrusted content
\end{description}

\medskip\noindent\textit{Notes:} Good paragraph pairing: cite Paper 4's limitation, then this paper as ``here is what a principled fix to that gap looks like at the model-training level, versus this project's guardrail-layer approach at inference time.''

\subsection{Paper 8 --- Judging LLM-as-a-Judge \cite{llmjudge2023}}\label{subsec:litreview-p8}

\begin{description}
\item[Full title] Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena
\item[Authors] Zheng, L., Chiang, W., Sheng, Y., Zhuang, S., Wu, Z., Zhuang, Y., Lin, Z., Li, Z., Li, D., Xing, E.P., Zhang, H., Gonzalez, J.E., Stoica, I.
\item[Year] 2023
\item[Venue] NeurIPS 2023, Datasets and Benchmarks Track
\item[URL/DOI] \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685}
\item[Method] Uses strong LLMs (e.g.\ GPT-4) as automated judges to score model responses to open-ended questions, then validates judge reliability against large-scale human preference data (MT-Bench, Chatbot Arena)
\item[Dataset] MT-Bench (multi-turn question set), 3K expert votes, 30K human-preference conversations from Chatbot Arena
\item[Key result] Strong LLM judges achieve over 80\% agreement with human preferences --- comparable to human-human agreement --- but exhibit documented biases: position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias, and limited reasoning ability on math/logic questions
\item[Limitation] Validated on general open-ended chat quality, not on a security-specific, high-stakes factual-verification task like classifying whether a CVE/ATT\&CK citation is grounded --- the documented biases (especially self-enhancement, if the same model family judges and generates) are a real risk to control for
\item[Relevance] Direct methodology source for the planned LLM-judge baseline (Aug 23 milestone) --- its documented bias list is exactly what to check for/report when using an LLM judge as a comparison point against this project's deterministic, database-grounded verification
\end{description}

\medskip\noindent\textit{Notes:} When implementing the LLM-judge baseline, deliberately use a different model family for the judge than for the SOC agent's own analysis, to avoid the self-enhancement bias this paper documents.

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $n \geq 0 \vee x \neq 0$
\Ensure $y = x^n$ 
\State $y \Leftarrow 1$
\If{$n < 0$}\label{algln2}
        \State $X \Leftarrow 1 / x$
        \State $N \Leftarrow -n$
\Else
        \State $X \Leftarrow x$
        \State $N \Leftarrow n$
\EndIf
\While{$N \neq 0$}
        \If{$N$ is even}
            \State $X \Leftarrow X \times X$
            \State $N \Leftarrow N / 2$
        \Else[$N$ is odd]
            \State $y \Leftarrow y \times X$
            \State $N \Leftarrow N - 1$
        \EndIf
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Cross referencing}\label{sec8}

Environments such as figure, table, equation and align can have a label
declared via the \verb+\label{#label}+ command. For figures and table
environments use the \verb+\label{}+ command inside or just
below the \verb+\caption{}+ command. You can then use the
\verb+\ref{#label}+ command to cross-reference them. As an example, consider
the label declared for Figure~\ref{fig1} which is
\verb+\label{fig1}+. To cross-reference it, use the command 
\verb+Figure \ref{fig1}+, for which it comes up as
``Figure~\ref{fig1}''. 

To reference line numbers in an algorithm, consider the label declared for the line number 2 of Algorithm~\ref{algo1} is \verb+\label{algln2}+. To cross-reference it, use the command \verb+\ref{algln2}+ for which it comes up as line~\ref{algln2} of Algorithm~\ref{algo1}.

\section{Methods}\label{sec11}

Topical subheadings are allowed. Authors must ensure that their Methods section includes adequate experimental and characterization data necessary for others in the field to reproduce their work. Authors are encouraged to include RIIDs where appropriate. 

\textbf{Ethical approval declarations} (only required where applicable) Any article reporting experiment/s carried out on (i)~live vertebrate (or higher invertebrates), (ii)~humans or (iii)~human samples must include an unambiguous statement within the methods section that meets the following requirements: 

\begin{enumerate}[1.]
\item Approval: a statement which confirms that all experimental protocols were approved by a named institutional and/or licensing committee. Please identify the approving body in the methods section

\item Accordance: a statement explicitly saying that the methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations

\item Informed consent (for experiments involving humans or human tissue samples): include a statement confirming that informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardian/s
\end{enumerate}

If your manuscript includes potentially identifying patient/participant information, or if it describes human transplantation research, or if it reports results of a clinical trial then  additional information will be required. Please visit (\url{https://www.nature.com/nature-research/editorial-policies}) for Nature Portfolio journals, (\url{https://www.springer.com/gp/authors-editors/journal-author/journal-author-helpdesk/publishing-ethics/14214}) for Springer Nature journals, or (\url{https://www.biomedcentral.com/getpublished/editorial-policies\#ethics+and+consent}) for BMC.
